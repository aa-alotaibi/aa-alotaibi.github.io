Why this line

The data came from a real heavy clay plant, recorded by its own MES. It covered the same line before and after a Lean Six Sigma program, which made it possible to ask a question plants often answer by feel: did the improvement work, and what should come next?

The first surprise

The headline numbers looked good: OEE was up, and breakdowns had dropped by about a quarter. But when I added up every unplanned stop, the line was losing almost as much time as before. The losses had moved, not disappeared. That changed the question from "did it improve?" to "where did the time go?"

At a glance

  • Public dataset from a real heavy clay plant (Zenodo record 17855209, Evocon MES)
  • Two periods: before and after Lean Six Sigma
  • Tools: Power BI, Power Query, statistical tests, Simio
  • Output: dashboard, validated simulation model, 10-page report

How I analyzed the plant data

I loaded the plant's shift and stop records into Power BI and prepared them in Power Query: correct data types, a Period field to split the two periods, and separate fact and date tables so both periods sat on the same basis. The dashboard then compares OEE, downtime and reliability indicators across the two periods.

I tested every important change before calling it real: a Welch t-test for OEE, a Mann–Whitney test for daily failures, and I-MR control charts for stability. The same records pointed to three critical stations, each for a different reason.

What the plant data said

  • OEE rose 5.2 points (p = 0.039)
  • Breakdowns fell about a quarter, but total unplanned stops fell only 3.9%
  • Dryer: fails most often
  • Hacking Station: longest repairs
  • Extruder Press: failures rose after LSS

Building the model

I rebuilt the line in Simio: nine machines in series from silo to packaging, about 6,000 bricks per hour, with failure and repair times taken from the plant's records.

Checking it against the plant

Before testing anything, I ran the Before-LSS and After-LSS models 30 times each and compared them with the plant. Every value landed within ±10%, so both models were frozen and used as they were.

What-If scenarios

Each scenario gave one, two or all three critical stations the same change: MTBF increased by 10% and MTTR reduced by 10%. Seven scenarios, 100 replications each.

These are simulation averages, not measured plant results. The Combined scenario gave the lowest average failure-related downtime. The gap between the best and worst scenarios (2.48 min/day) is smaller than the run-to-run variation (95% confidence half-widths of about 6.4 to 7.4 min/day), so the ranking is not proof that one scenario is statistically better than another. It is an order of action, not a promised saving.

Where to start

Combined gave the lowest average, but it means changing three stations at once. Hacking Station alone came closest of any single station, so it is the recommended starting point for a practical pilot.

PHASE 1 · START HERE

Hacking Station

Shorten the long repairs: spare parts ready and clear escalation. Best single-station result in the simulation (37.84 min/day).

PHASE 2

Extruder Press

Fewer failures: preventive maintenance and root-cause work on repeat stops. Together with Hacking, the strongest pair (37.71 min/day).

PHASE 3 · IF JUSTIFIED

Dryer

Condition monitoring and inspections, once its benefit can be weighed against its cost.

Decisions along the way

Keep real and simulated apart

Every chart and sentence says which one it comes from, because mixing them is the easiest way to overstate a result.

Validate before testing

I compared the model with the plant first, then froze it, so the What-If results could not be tuned to look better.

Rank, don't promise

The gaps between scenarios were smaller than the model's own variation, so I present them as an order of action, not as guaranteed savings.

What the model can't do

The simulation only covers breakdowns. It doesn't see speed losses, quality, or the handling and rack stops that grew after LSS. Failure times use real averages, but their shape was assumed. And without cost data, it can say where to start, not whether it pays.

If I did it again

I would ask for cost data at the start, so the recommendation could end in a number, not a rule. I would also collect more repair records to fit the failure distributions properly, and look at the non-breakdown stops earlier, since that is where most of the lost time now sits.
